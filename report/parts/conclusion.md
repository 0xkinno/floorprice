Seven ways of scoring a source differ by orders of magnitude in what they cost to cheat. In simulation, an accuracy record falls to {{hypotheses.H5.con_T6a_median_articles}} articles and a majority vote to {{hypotheses.H6.majority_median_accounts}} accounts, while bridging needs {{hypotheses.H6.bridging_median_accounts}}. On four real language models, {{llm.summary.E3_total_crossed}} of {{llm.summary.E3_total_trials}} page-text trials and {{llm.summary.E2_prestige_crossed}} of {{llm.summary.E2_prestige_trials}} prestige-name trials crossed the models' own reliable bar, so the pre-registered expectation that they could be bought was wrong. Instruction injection did not help the attacker.

Resisting liars also costs honest newcomers time: the techniques hardest to cheat are the slowest to credit an honest source, and the cheapest to attack credit newcomers quickly. A score should therefore be reported with both numbers, its floor price and its honest wait, and with a receipt that lets an outsider recompute it. The composite rule, with a lower-only model veto and bridging-gated resolutions, is a concrete design that never lets the model raise a score; its simulated price is the bridging price and its honest wait is that of the track record alone. Testing it on real claim resolutions is the next step.
